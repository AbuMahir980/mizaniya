# Hierarchy, and why attention is a budget

**The one line:** attention and colour are finite. Every extra thing you
emphasise makes everything else quieter.

---

## What it is

A screen has a fixed amount of *notice* to hand out. So does a colour. Neither
is created by adding more — adding more only divides what is already there.

Two consequences, and they are the same rule:

| Resource | Spend it badly and… |
|---|---|
| **Position and size** on a screen | Nine equal tiles means the important one is no longer important |
| **The danger colour** | Red on a delete button means red on a negative balance says nothing |

---

## Why it decides Home's layout

Mizaniya's Home could have shown the spreadsheet's eight dashboard figures as
eight equal tiles. That is the honest translation of the source — and it would
have been the spreadsheet rendered smaller, which is what a spreadsheet is
already better at.

Instead the screen is **ranked** (D8):

1. one hero — *safe to spend today*
2. four tiles
3. a banner that appears only when it has something to say
4. two tables

The test for anything new: **"what does this displace?"** A screen where
everything is emphasised has no emphasis at all, so a ninth tile does not cost
nothing — it costs a little of each of the other eight, and a little of the
hero.

That is also why the unallocated figure is a **banner that disappears at ₦0**
rather than a tile that permanently reads zero. A tile that is always zero
teaches you to stop looking at that part of the screen — and the habit does not
switch off on the day it finally has a number in it.

---

## Why the offline note is not red

Red in this app means **money going wrong**: negative safe-to-spend, an
overspent envelope, a missed schedule (the addendum, **F7**). Nothing else.

Being offline is not money going wrong. Neither is deleting a transaction you
typed twice, nor a refused import that changed nothing.

Colour red on those and you have not made them clearer — you have made the
*real* red weaker. The owner learns that red appears routinely, stops reacting
to it, and then one day the figure that actually matters turns red and gets the
same shrug as the offline banner.

**Spending a signal on ordinary things is how you lose the signal.**

---

## What we would have done instead, and why not

**Show everything equally and let the owner decide what matters.** It sounds
respectful. It moves the work onto the person opening the app in a hurry, which
is the moment they have least capacity for it. The app knows which figure they
came for; refusing to say so is not neutrality, it is abdication.

**Use red for anything urgent-ish.** Then "urgent" becomes the normal state of
the screen, and there is nowhere left to go when something genuinely is.

---

## The interview sentence

"I treat emphasis as a budget — a screen only has so much attention and a
warning colour only has so much force, so I rank deliberately and reserve the
danger colour for one meaning, because spending it on ordinary states is how you
lose it."
